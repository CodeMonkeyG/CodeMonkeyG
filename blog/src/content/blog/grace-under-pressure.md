Lately my feed has started to sound the same. Engineers with decades of experience describing hundreds of applications and a handful of replies. People questioning whether interviews measure anything real. Posts that begin as observations and end somewhere closer to anger. I recognize the feeling in every one of them, because I have felt it too.

This market is hard in a way that is difficult to explain to anyone outside of it. Capable people are being filtered out before a human ever reads their name. The interview process often asks for a polished story about a problem solved years ago rather than evidence of how someone thinks today. Silence has become the default response. If you are in the middle of this and you feel that something is broken, you are reading the situation correctly.

What I want to write about is not the brokenness. Plenty of people are documenting that, and they are not wrong. I want to write about what we do with the frustration, because that part is still ours.

## The Feeling Is Earned

I do not think anyone should pretend this is fine. Losing a role you spent years building, or watching a search stretch from weeks into months, carries real weight. It affects how you sleep, how you talk to the people around you, and how you see your own work. The sense that the rules changed while you were busy doing the job is not imagined. The rules did change.

There is also a particular kind of hurt in being evaluated by a process you believe is measuring the wrong thing. You know what you can do. You have done it for a long time. And then a thirty minute conversation reduces all of it to whether you remembered the right anecdote. That gap between what you know about yourself and what the process can see is where most of the frustration lives.

I say all of this because the rest of what I want to say depends on it. Grace under pressure is not the same as denying the pressure. You cannot carry something well if you will not admit it is heavy.

## What the Pressure Reveals

Here is the thought I keep coming back to. The job search is not a pause before the work begins. It is the first assignment, and everyone involved is watching how we handle it.

Every rejection email is a small failed deploy. Every company that goes silent is a teammate who dropped the ball. Every interview that asks the wrong question is a stakeholder who does not understand the problem yet. None of these situations are new to anyone who has worked in software for a while. We have all handled versions of them inside a job, usually with more patience than we are showing now, because inside a job we understood that how we responded mattered.

It still matters. A hiring manager who reads a bitter post is not going to separate it from the engineer on the résumé, because they are the same person. The way someone talks about a company that ghosted them is a preview of how they will talk about a colleague who missed a deadline. The way someone handles not knowing an answer in an interview is a preview of how they will write an incident report when the root cause is embarrassing. Employers may not be scoring any of this deliberately. They see it anyway. It leaks into the tone of a follow up email, the framing of a LinkedIn update, and the way a candidate describes their last role.

That is not an argument for performing cheerfulness. False positivity is its own kind of dishonesty, and most experienced people can spot it quickly. It is an argument for deciding what the frustration turns into before it decides for you.

## Lashing Out Costs More Than It Returns

I understand the impulse to post the angry version. It feels like the honest version. It gets engagement, and for a moment it feels like being heard.

The problem is not that the anger is unjustified. The problem is that a public post outlives the moment that produced it. Six months from now, when the market has shifted or a recruiter is doing a last check before an offer, that post is still there, and it no longer reads as a reasonable reaction to a hard week. It reads as a pattern. The person who wrote it has moved on. The words have not.

There is also a quieter cost. Lashing out keeps you in the problem. It rehearses the frustration instead of processing it. Every time you restate how unfair the process is, you make the unfairness a little more central to how you see yourself, and that shows up in the next interview whether you intend it to or not.

The alternative is not silence. It is restraint. You can say the process is flawed without making it personal. You can describe what you have learned about interviewing without attacking the interviewers. You can be honest that the search has been hard and still speak about your last company, your last manager, and the roles that passed on you with a steady voice. That steadiness is noticed, and it is rare right now, which makes it more valuable than it has ever been.

## Carrying It Well

For me, carrying the pressure well has meant pointing the energy somewhere that would still be useful whether or not any particular application went anywhere. I went back through my own work and got stories of work experience down to tight anecdotes. I picked up programming languages and frameworks I had put off. I built things that nobody asked for, partly in order to stay sharp and partly because building is a better place to put frustration than a comment thread. I started this blog, in part, so that I would have somewhere to think out loud that was not a reaction to the latest rejection.

I am not suggesting that everyone needs to do the same things. Some people need rest more than they need another project. Some have responsibilities that leave no room for side work at all. The specific activity matters less than the posture behind it: facing a situation you did not choose, acknowledging it honestly, and still conducting yourself like someone you would want to work with.

That is what I mean by grace. Not calm for the sake of appearances, and not pretending the market is fair. Just the discipline to let the hard part of this season reveal the best of how you work instead of the worst of how you feel.

The market will do what it does. How we carry ourselves through it is the one part of this that is entirely ours, and it will still be true about us long after this stretch is over.
