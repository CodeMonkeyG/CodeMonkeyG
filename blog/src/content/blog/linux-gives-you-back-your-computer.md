For the last eight years, I have been deeply invested in Linux. During that time, I have moved between a couple of distributions, tested different desktop environments, changed the way I work, and watched Linux become a more capable and polished operating system. It is not perfect, but it gives me something Windows increasingly takes away: control over my own computer.

That matters when stories surface about VLC taking up to 33 seconds to open an MP3 on some Windows systems. According to the video, the problem may have involved Microsoft Defender interfering with VLC's startup files. VLC itself had not suddenly become poorly written. A Windows security layer may have been scanning or blocking files the application needed to start normally.

The proposed workaround was to tell Defender to ignore VLC by adding an exclusion. That might solve the immediate problem, but it also puts the user in the position of weakening a security setting just to make independent software function properly. Then, waiting in the background, is Microsoft's own media player, ready to become the supposedly obvious replacement.

The problem is not limited to VLC. The video also discusses reports involving Paint.NET, Windows updates, Edge, Microsoft Store behavior, default applications, and other parts of the Windows experience. Each problem can be explained away as a bug, a bad update, or an unfortunate interaction between components. After enough of these incidents, though, users start to notice the pattern: Microsoft controls the operating system, the security tools, the defaults, the store, and competing applications.

That does not prove Microsoft deliberately targeted VLC. A faulty detection rule or poorly tested update could explain what happened. Intent is not the only concern. When the company that controls the platform causes an independent application to misbehave, then offers its own software as the convenient alternative, users are still placed at a disadvantage.

Linux has given me a different relationship with my computer. I can choose a distribution, install the software I want, change the desktop, replace system components, select my defaults, and decide how updates should work. I can experiment without feeling like someone is constantly watching over the machine and waiting to redirect me toward a store, a browser, an account, or a subscription.

I have bounced between distros because Linux lets me do that. The experience has evolved considerably over the years, and modern Linux systems are far more accessible than many people assume. There are still hardware quirks and occasional configuration headaches, but those are usually problems I can investigate and solve. I am not constantly fighting a platform that wants to make decisions for me.

That freedom is why Linux remains important. A computer should not be an advertising channel or a locked storefront disguised as a personal device. It should be something the owner can understand, modify, repair, and use without being pushed toward the platform company's preferred products. After eight years with Linux, I do not see it as merely an alternative operating system. I see it as a way to take ownership of the PC experience again.
