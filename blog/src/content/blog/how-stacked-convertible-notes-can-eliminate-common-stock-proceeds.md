> **General information only.** This article describes common financing mechanics and an illustrative acquisition waterfall. Actual outcomes depend on the company's charter, financing documents, debt agreements, applicable law, taxes, and transaction terms. It is not legal, tax, or investment advice. A stockholder considering an inspection demand or transaction should consult qualified counsel.

Multiple rounds of convertible notes without a priced equity round can create a substantial **liquidation overhang**: claims that must be resolved before common stock participates in an acquisition. In a modest exit, the acquisition headline can look healthy while the amount available to common stockholders is small or zero.

The result is not automatic. It depends on the precise note agreements, capitalization, senior debt, liquidation-preference terms, and merger agreement. But the mechanics are straightforward enough that every stockholder should understand the waterfall.

## 1. Unconverted notes may sit ahead of equity

A convertible note begins as debt. If an acquisition occurs before a qualifying financing causes conversion, the note agreement generally determines whether the holder is repaid, converts into shares, or may choose between those outcomes.

Where repayment applies, principal and accrued interest are claims that must be satisfied from transaction consideration before distributions to equity holders. Existing secured debt, transaction expenses, taxes, and other liabilities may rank ahead as well. Preferred and common stockholders do not share in the residual until those obligations are resolved.

This is why the label "convertible" should not obscure the underlying risk: until conversion, a note can function as a senior claim on exit proceeds.

## 2. Change-of-control provisions can increase the claim

Convertible-note agreements commonly include a change-of-control provision for an acquisition before a qualified financing. The exact language varies, but it may give the holder a choice between:

- repayment of outstanding principal plus interest, sometimes with a premium or multiple;
- conversion immediately before closing at a discount, valuation cap, or other formula; or
- another contractually defined treatment.

A multiple has a direct effect on the waterfall. For example, $5 million of principal subject to a 2.0x cash payout would require $10 million of consideration before the remaining distribution is shared with equity holders, subject to the agreement and any senior claims. That is very different from a conversion that turns the noteholder into an equity participant.

The governing documents, not an assumed market norm, control. A cap, discount, maturity date, interest rate, and change-of-control election should all be reviewed together.

## 3. Interest adds to the overhang over time

Notes usually accrue interest until they are repaid or converted. The longer a company delays a priced financing or exit, the greater the repayment amount may become.

As a simplified example, $3 million accruing simple interest at 7% annually reaches approximately $4.05 million after five years and $4.26 million after six years, before any change-of-control premium. Whether interest compounds, converts, or is paid in cash depends on the note.

## An illustrative acquisition waterfall

Assume a company completed a $2.4 million seed preferred round and later issued $6 million of convertible notes. The following example assumes the notes are repaid for $7.5 million including accrued interest, the seed investors receive a $2.4 million non-participating liquidation preference, and there are no other senior claims beyond those shown.

| Step | Distribution | Remaining proceeds |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Acquisition sale price | $15,000,000 | $15,000,000 |
| Transaction and legal fees | -$1,000,000 | $14,000,000 |
| Convertible notes: principal and interest | -$7,500,000 | $6,500,000 |
| Seed preferred liquidation preference | -$2,400,000 | $4,100,000 |
| Corporate liabilities and taxes | -$1,000,000 | $3,100,000 |

Under those assumptions, only $3.1 million remains for common stock and any other participating equity. If the notes instead have a 2.0x cash change-of-control payment based on $6 million of principal, the note claim alone becomes $12 million. With the same fees, preference, and liabilities, the proceeds available for common stock are reduced to zero.

This is only a model, not a valuation conclusion. A real waterfall can change materially if notes convert, preferred holders elect conversion, preferences are participating or stacked, debt is secured, option holders participate, escrow or indemnity holdbacks apply, or consideration includes stock rather than cash.

## Why the capitalization records matter

Public fundraising filings may show an early equity round but not the full financing picture. The cap table and note or SAFE register are the practical records needed to understand the senior stack.

For a Delaware corporation, a properly scoped inspection demand under DGCL Section 220 may be a way for a stockholder with a proper purpose to seek relevant books and records. Counsel can advise on entitlement, purpose, scope, and the appropriate recipient. Useful records to request may include:

- the current and historical capitalization table, including fully diluted ownership;
- every outstanding note and SAFE, together with amendments and side letters;
- principal amounts, interest rates, maturity dates, valuation caps, discounts, and change-of-control terms;
- the certificate of incorporation and each preferred-stock financing document; and
- board materials or transaction analyses addressing an acquisition or recapitalization, where appropriately within scope.

## Establish the senior-claim floor

Once the records are available, the analysis starts with a simple question: **how much consideration must be paid or allocated before common stock receives anything?**

Build the calculation from the executed documents:

1. Identify secured debt, working-capital adjustments, fees, taxes, and other non-equity obligations.
2. Calculate each convertible instrument's repayment amount, accrued interest, and change-of-control treatment.
3. Model conversion outcomes using the relevant cap, discount, and capitalization definitions.
4. Apply preferred-stock liquidation preferences and conversion elections.
5. Compare the resulting residual with the total common shares and options entitled to participate.

The result is a break-even threshold: the transaction value at which common stock begins to have a meaningful cash outcome. Knowing that threshold is essential before relying on a headline acquisition valuation or assuming that a large common-share position will translate into proceeds.

## Conclusion

Convertible financing can be useful when it bridges a company to a priced round. The risk arises when notes accumulate for years without conversion and then meet a sale price that is insufficient to satisfy debt-like claims, premiums, accrued interest, and preferred preferences.

For common stockholders, the important work is documentary rather than speculative: obtain the governing records through appropriate channels, model the contractual waterfall carefully, and obtain legal and financial advice before drawing conclusions about an exit.
